# Ameen User Guide

Status: Draft v1.0  
Owner: Product / Customer Success  
Audience: Compliance users, control owners, evidence reviewers

---

## Purpose

This guide explains how a normal customer user should use Ameen during an assessment or pilot.

The user should not need to understand technical architecture. The user only needs to understand the workflow.

---

## The Ameen Workflow

Ameen follows a simple journey:

1. Upload documents.
2. Review AI suggestions.
3. Confirm control mappings.
4. Add missing evidence.
5. Review readiness.
6. Generate reports.

The platform is designed to reduce manual tracking and make compliance easier to understand.

---

## Step 1: Upload Documents

Upload documents that may support the selected compliance framework.

Examples include:

- Information security policies.
- Access control policies.
- Risk management procedures.
- Incident response plans.
- Business continuity plans.
- Vendor management policies.
- Audit reports.
- Evidence files.
- Screenshots or exports from internal systems.

Only upload documents that are approved for the assessment scope.

---

## Step 2: Let Ameen Analyze

After upload, Ameen analyzes the document and suggests where it may support controls.

The result may include:

- Suggested control matches.
- Confidence level.
- Explanation.
- Relevant text signals.
- Recommended next actions.

A suggestion is not final approval.

---

## Step 3: Review Suggestions

The user should review every suggestion before accepting it.

Ask:

- Does the document really address this control?
- Is the statement specific enough?
- Is it only a policy statement, or is there evidence of implementation?
- Does the evidence belong to the correct organization and period?
- Is anything missing?

Accept only what you can defend.

---

## Step 4: Add Evidence

If Ameen shows that a control is missing evidence, upload or attach supporting artifacts.

Good evidence should be:

- Relevant.
- Current.
- Specific.
- Traceable.
- Owned by the right department.
- Approved for use in the assessment.

Poor evidence is generic, outdated, unrelated, or impossible to verify.

---

## Step 5: Understand Readiness

Ameen separates readiness into four dimensions:

- Documented: There is a document saying what should happen.
- Implemented: There is indication that the control exists in practice.
- Evidenced: Supporting proof has been attached.
- Verified: A human reviewer has accepted the evidence.

This prevents a common mistake: treating a written policy as full compliance.

---

## Step 6: Use the Dashboard

The readiness dashboard helps users understand:

- Overall readiness.
- Strong areas.
- Weak areas.
- Missing evidence.
- Pending reviews.
- High-priority gaps.
- Improvement over time.

The dashboard should be used for internal decision-making and preparation.

---

## Step 7: Generate Reports

Reports should be generated after the assessment scope and evidence have been reviewed.

Reports should not be sent externally without internal approval.

Ameen reports are readiness reports. They are not official regulatory certificates or final audit opinions.

---

## User Best Practices

Use Ameen effectively by following these rules:

- Upload focused documents, not every file available.
- Review AI suggestions carefully.
- Do not accept weak mappings just to improve the score.
- Add comments when rejecting suggestions.
- Keep evidence current.
- Assign ownership for missing items.
- Use the dashboard for prioritization.

---

## Common Mistakes

Avoid these mistakes:

- Uploading documents outside scope.
- Treating AI suggestions as final approval.
- Accepting a control because one keyword appears in a document.
- Confusing documentation with implementation.
- Using old evidence without confirming validity.
- Exporting reports before review.

---

## Human Review Principle

Ameen helps you move faster, but the organization remains responsible for compliance decisions.

The safest rule is simple:

Do not approve anything in Ameen that you would not be comfortable explaining to an internal auditor, external advisor, or executive committee.
